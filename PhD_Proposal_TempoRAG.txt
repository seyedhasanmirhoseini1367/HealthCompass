================================================================================
ABSTRACT
================================================================================

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) has emerged as a principal architecture
for grounding large language model (LLM) outputs in factual, domain-specific
knowledge, including within healthcare applications (Lewis et al., 2020;
Gao et al., 2024). However, existing healthcare RAG systems suffer from a
fundamental limitation: they treat every retrieved clinical document as a
temporally flat, semantically interchangeable unit. Medical knowledge is
inherently longitudinal — a patient's health trajectory, defined by the
sequential evolution of laboratory values, medication changes, diagnoses, and
clinical observations across time, carries diagnostic and prognostic meaning
that cannot be captured by similarity-based retrieval of isolated text
fragments. This proposal presents TempoRAG, a novel framework for Temporal
Clinical Trajectory-Aware Retrieval-Augmented Generation in Electronic Health
Record (EHR) question answering. TempoRAG introduces three core contributions:
(1) a temporal chunk graph representation that encodes clinical records as
ordered nodes with explicit temporal relations; (2) a trajectory-aware retrieval
mechanism capable of retrieving ordered clinical sequences rather than ranked
isolated chunks; and (3) a new benchmark dataset, ClinicalTrajQA, constructed
from the MIMIC-IV EHR dataset (Johnson et al., 2023), comprising question-
answer pairs requiring temporal trajectory reasoning. This research addresses
a critical gap at the intersection of clinical natural language processing,
temporal reasoning, and responsible AI deployment in healthcare settings.


================================================================================
1. INTRODUCTION
================================================================================

The adoption of large language models in healthcare has accelerated
significantly, with systems demonstrating near-expert performance on medical
licensing examinations and clinical question answering (Singhal et al., 2023;
Nori et al., 2023). Retrieval-Augmented Generation, first formalised by Lewis
et al. (2020), augments language models with external knowledge retrieved at
inference time, mitigating hallucination and enabling the use of private,
patient-specific information that was not included in pre-training corpora.
Healthcare organisations have begun deploying RAG systems over Electronic Health
Records (EHRs) to support clinical decision-making, patient self-service, and
administrative workflows (Zakka et al., 2024; Moor et al., 2023).

Despite these advances, current healthcare RAG systems share a structural flaw
that has not been formally identified or addressed in the research literature:
they perform retrieval by ranking document chunks according to semantic
similarity to the query, then pass the highest-ranked chunks to a language model
for generation. This paradigm implicitly assumes that clinical knowledge is
static and can be evaluated by similarity alone. In reality, the most clinically
significant information is often encoded not in any single record but in the
trajectory — the pattern of change across multiple records ordered in time.

Consider a clinician or patient asking: "Has my kidney function been improving
or worsening over the past year?" A standard RAG system will retrieve the
chunks most semantically similar to the query. This may include the most recent
creatinine measurement and perhaps one or two earlier values — but the system
has no mechanism to (a) ensure complete temporal coverage of the relevant
period, (b) order retrieved chunks chronologically, (c) represent the direction
or rate of change, or (d) generate a response that accurately characterises the
trajectory rather than summarising isolated data points.

This represents a fundamental research gap with direct patient safety
implications. Incorrect or incomplete temporal reasoning about clinical
trajectories can mislead patients about the progression of chronic conditions,
obscure drug response patterns, misrepresent recovery trajectories following
surgery, and fail to surface deteriorating trends that warrant urgent clinical
attention.

This proposal addresses this gap through the design, implementation, and
rigorous evaluation of TempoRAG: a trajectory-aware RAG framework for EHR
question answering. The work contributes to the broader agenda of developing
safe, clinically reliable, and epistemically honest AI systems for healthcare,
responding to calls in the literature for foundation models that are genuinely
trustworthy in high-stakes medical environments (Wornow et al., 2023;
Rajpurkar et al., 2022).


================================================================================
2. BACKGROUND AND LITERATURE REVIEW
================================================================================

2.1 Retrieval-Augmented Generation

Lewis et al. (2020) introduced RAG as a sequence-to-sequence architecture that
combines a non-parametric retrieval component (dense passage retrieval over an
external corpus) with a parametric generative language model. The system
conditions generation on retrieved passages, enabling factual grounding without
full model fine-tuning. Subsequent work has substantially extended this
framework, including hybrid sparse-dense retrieval, reranking strategies, and
multi-hop retrieval pipelines (Gao et al., 2024).

Gao et al. (2024) provide a comprehensive survey of RAG variants, categorising
approaches along the dimensions of retrieval granularity (token, passage,
document), retrieval timing (single-step, iterative, adaptive), and retrieval-
generation interaction. The survey identifies evaluation as a significant
challenge, a concern later addressed by the RAGAS framework (Es et al., 2024),
which provides reference-free metrics for faithfulness, answer relevancy, and
context precision in RAG pipelines.

A critical observation for this proposal: none of the RAG architectures surveyed
by Gao et al. (2024) incorporates temporal ordering as a first-class retrieval
criterion. Retrieval is universally framed as a ranking problem over semantically
similar units, not as a sequencing problem over temporally ordered clinical
events.

2.2 Healthcare Applications of Large Language Models

Singhal et al. (2023) demonstrated that large language models can encode
substantial clinical knowledge, with the Med-PaLM system achieving expert-level
performance on the United States Medical Licensing Examination (USMLE). This
landmark result stimulated rapid development of medical AI systems, including
GPT-4's demonstrated capabilities on clinical challenge problems (Nori et al.,
2023). Moor et al. (2023) articulated the vision of generalist medical AI
through foundation models pre-trained on multimodal medical data, while
Wornow et al. (2023) offered a cautionary assessment of the "shaky foundations"
of clinical language models, highlighting gaps in evaluation rigour, temporal
generalisability, and deployment safety.

Healthcare-specific RAG systems have been developed to provide factual grounding
for medical AI outputs. Zakka et al. (2024) introduced Almanac, a RAG system
for clinical medicine that retrieves from curated medical knowledge bases,
demonstrating measurable improvements in factual accuracy over ungrounded
generation. Xiong et al. (2024) established a systematic benchmark for medical
RAG, revealing that performance varies substantially across clinical domains and
query types, with temporal queries performing markedly worse than atemporal
factual lookups — a finding directly motivating the present proposal.

2.3 Domain-Specific Language Models for Clinical Text

Pre-training language models on clinical corpora has produced substantial
improvements in biomedical NLP tasks. Gu et al. (2021) introduced PubMedBERT,
trained exclusively on PubMed abstracts, demonstrating that domain-specific
pre-training outperforms general-domain models on biomedical benchmarks even
when the general-domain model is substantially larger. Rasmy et al. (2021)
extended this to structured EHR data with Med-BERT, pre-training on
longitudinal patient records from a large healthcare network. Alsentzer et al.
(2019) specifically addressed clinical text (nursing notes, discharge summaries,
radiology reports) with ClinicalBERT, trained on the MIMIC-III corpus. These
models provide a strong foundation for embedding clinical record content but
are not designed to encode temporal position or trajectory information.

2.4 Temporal Reasoning in Clinical Natural Language Processing

Temporal reasoning in clinical NLP has a well-established research history.
Styler et al. (2014) introduced the THYME corpus — a gold-standard temporal
annotation resource for clinical text containing explicit BEFORE, AFTER,
SIMULTANEOUS, and CONTAINS temporal relations between clinical events and time
expressions. This corpus, built upon the MIMIC-III and Mayo Clinic datasets,
provided the foundation for the Clinical TempEval shared task series.

Lin et al. (2019) demonstrated that BERT-based models could achieve strong
performance on within-sentence and cross-sentence temporal relation extraction
from clinical notes, outperforming earlier feature-engineered approaches. Viani
et al. (2021) applied NLP techniques to mining temporal disease trajectories
from healthcare records, identifying patterns of disease onset, progression,
and co-morbidity development. However, this body of work focuses on extracting
temporal relations from individual documents; it does not address retrieval
across temporally ordered sequences of clinical records in a RAG framework.

2.5 Longitudinal EHR Modelling

The machine learning for healthcare literature has extensively studied
longitudinal EHR modelling for prediction tasks. Choi et al. (2016) introduced
Doctor AI, an LSTM-based model trained on sequences of clinical codes to predict
future diagnoses and medications. Rasmy et al. (2021) extended this paradigm
with transformer-based architectures pre-trained on structured clinical event
sequences. Luo et al. (2020) developed HiTANet, a hierarchical time-aware
attention network for risk prediction from EHR sequences.

A critical distinction separates this predictive modelling literature from the
present proposal: these systems are trained to predict structured clinical
outputs (ICD codes, medication orders) from structured inputs (coded diagnoses,
lab values). They do not support natural language question answering, do not use
a retrieval mechanism, and do not expose their reasoning in a format accessible
to non-expert users. TempoRAG addresses the complementary problem: enabling
natural language temporal reasoning over longitudinal clinical records through
a retrieval-augmented generation framework.

2.6 Graph-Based Retrieval Augmentation

Recent work has explored graph-structured representations for RAG, moving beyond
flat corpora of text chunks. Edge et al. (2024) introduced GraphRAG, which
constructs a knowledge graph from a document corpus and performs retrieval by
graph traversal rather than dense vector similarity. This enables retrieval of
structurally related entities and relationships rather than merely semantically
similar text segments. Applying graph-structured retrieval to temporal clinical
records — where the relevant graph structure is a temporal sequence of clinical
events specific to an individual patient — is a direction not addressed by the
current GraphRAG literature, which focuses on topical rather than temporal
relationships.

2.7 Summary of Research Gap

Synthesising the above literature reveals a gap at the intersection of three
research areas: (1) healthcare RAG systems, which provide clinical grounding for
language models but treat records as temporally flat; (2) clinical temporal NLP,
which extracts temporal relations from individual documents but does not
integrate with retrieval-generation pipelines; and (3) longitudinal EHR
modelling, which captures temporal patterns for prediction tasks but does not
support natural language querying. TempoRAG is designed to bridge these three
research traditions within a unified, clinically validated framework.

3. PROBLEM STATEMENT
================================================================================

Current healthcare RAG systems fail to reason correctly about clinical
trajectories — the ordered, longitudinal sequences of clinical events that
constitute a patient's health history. This failure manifests in three
observable ways:

(a) Temporal Incompleteness: Similarity-based retrieval does not guarantee
    retrieval of all records relevant to a specified time interval. Records that
    are clinically relevant to a trajectory question (e.g., a lab value from
    eight months ago that represents the trend baseline) may rank below the
    similarity threshold if their individual content is less semantically similar
    to the query than records from a different period.

(b) Temporal Disorder: Retrieved chunks are presented to the generation model
    ranked by relevance score, not chronological order. The LLM therefore
    receives a temporally disordered context, making it systematically difficult
    to produce coherent trend descriptions, comparisons, or duration estimates.

(c) Trajectory Blindness: No existing RAG retrieval mechanism explicitly
    encodes or retrieves temporal relations (BEFORE, AFTER, DURING) between
    clinical records. The system cannot retrieve "the sequence of lab results
    leading up to the patient's hospitalisation" as a unit — it can only
    retrieve individual records that happen to be semantically similar to a
    query about that sequence.

These limitations are not addressed by standard improvements to RAG (better
embeddings, improved reranking, or larger context windows), because they are
architectural rather than parametric: the retrieval schema itself lacks temporal
structure.


================================================================================
4. RESEARCH QUESTIONS
================================================================================

This research is organised around four primary research questions:

RQ1: How can clinical EHR records be represented as temporal structures that
     preserve ordering, duration, and inter-record temporal relations, in a form
     compatible with vector retrieval?

RQ2: What query classification approach can reliably distinguish temporally-
     sensitive clinical queries (trend, comparison, duration, causality) from
     atemporal point-in-time queries, enabling adaptive retrieval strategies?

RQ3: Can a trajectory-aware retrieval mechanism — one that retrieves ordered
     clinical sequences rather than ranked individual chunks — produce
     measurably better temporal question answering outcomes compared to
     standard RAG baselines?

RQ4: How can the quality of temporal trajectory reasoning in healthcare RAG
     systems be rigorously evaluated, and what new benchmarks are required to
     do so reliably?

5. PROPOSED METHODOLOGY
================================================================================

The TempoRAG framework is organised into five interconnected components,
corresponding to five phases of the research programme.

5.1 Phase 1: Temporal Chunk Graph Construction

Standard RAG indexes a document corpus as a flat set of text chunks, each
associated with a dense vector embedding. TempoRAG replaces this flat index
with a directed temporal graph G = (V, E) where:

    V = {v_i} is the set of clinical chunk nodes. Each node v_i encodes:
        - content: the text of the chunk (as in standard RAG)
        - content_embedding: a dense vector representation of the text
        - temporal_embedding: a dense vector encoding the chunk's position in
          the patient's longitudinal record (detailed below)
        - timestamp: the calendar date of the associated clinical event
        - record_type: the clinical category (laboratory, medication, imaging,
          clinical note, wearable, discharge summary)
        - patient_id: the patient to whom this record belongs

    E = {(v_i, v_j, r)} is the set of directed temporal relation edges, where
        r is drawn from the relation set {BEFORE, AFTER, SIMULTANEOUS, DURING,
        BEGINS, ENDS}, following the TimeML annotation standard (Styler et al.,
        2014). Edges are constructed automatically from timestamps and, for
        clinical notes, from temporal relation extraction models trained on the
        THYME corpus.

Temporal embeddings are constructed using a time-aware positional encoding
adapted from clinical sequence modelling literature (Rasmy et al., 2021). The
encoding represents each chunk's position relative to three reference frames:
(a) absolute calendar time (year, month, day); (b) relative time within the
patient's complete medical history (normalised position between first and last
recorded event); and (c) relative time from the most recent record (recency).
These three signals are projected through a learned linear layer to produce a
fixed-dimensional temporal embedding that is concatenated with the content
embedding at retrieval time.

The temporal chunk graph is constructed per patient and stored in a graph
database (Neo4j) to enable efficient traversal queries alongside vector
similarity search.

5.2 Phase 2: Trajectory-Aware Query Classification

Before retrieval, incoming natural language queries are classified into one of
five query types:

    Type 1 — Point-in-time: "What is my current LDL cholesterol level?"
              → Standard similarity-based retrieval (no trajectory logic needed)

    Type 2 — Trend: "Is my blood pressure getting better or worse?"
              → Trajectory retrieval over the relevant lab/vital sign type,
                ordered chronologically, covering the query's implied time span

    Type 3 — Comparison: "How do my kidney function values compare between
              last year and this year?"
              → Dual-window retrieval: retrieve the relevant record type from
                two specified time periods and present both sequences

    Type 4 — Duration: "How long have I been taking metformin?"
              → Span retrieval: retrieve the start event (medication prescribed)
                and track forward through any modification events (dose change,
                discontinuation)

    Type 5 — Causal-temporal: "Did my cholesterol improve after I started
              the statin?"
              → Intervention retrieval: retrieve the medication event and the
                target biomarker trajectories pre- and post-intervention

Classification is performed by a fine-tuned BERT-based classifier (Gu et al.,
2021) trained on a labelled query dataset constructed from clinical QA pairs
annotated by domain experts. A lightweight (BERT-base) model is used to ensure
low-latency classification suitable for interactive deployment.

5.3 Phase 3: Trajectory Retrieval Architecture

For non-point-in-time queries (Types 2-5), standard similarity-based retrieval
is replaced or augmented by trajectory retrieval, a graph traversal and
alignment procedure defined as follows:

Step 1 — Temporal Anchor Identification: From the query, extract the relevant
clinical domain (e.g., creatinine, blood pressure, statin medication) and the
implied time window (e.g., "past year," "since 2024," "before my surgery").
A lightweight named entity recognition model identifies clinical entities, and a
temporal expression normaliser (based on the SUTime framework) maps natural
language time expressions to calendar intervals.

Step 2 — Subgraph Extraction: Query the temporal chunk graph for all nodes v_i
belonging to the target patient, within the identified clinical domain and time
window. This produces a subgraph G_q ⊆ G that contains exactly the records
relevant to the query's temporal scope.

Step 3 — Trajectory Sequence Construction: Perform a topological sort of G_q
along the BEFORE/AFTER edge relations to produce an ordered sequence
S = [v_1, v_2, ..., v_n] where each v_k temporally precedes v_{k+1}. Where
temporal relations are uncertain (e.g., two records from the same day), order
by timestamp and record type according to clinical convention (lab results
before clinical notes on the same day, reflecting the typical diagnostic flow).

Step 4 — Temporal MMR: Apply a temporally adapted variant of Maximal Marginal
Relevance (Carbonell & Goldstein, 1998) to the sequence S to select a subset
of chunks that maximises both content relevance and temporal diversity —
ensuring that the selected chunks span the time window rather than clustering
around a single date. The temporal MMR objective balances content similarity
to the query, temporal diversity (coverage of the relevant period), and
sequential coherence (preserving the ordering of the original trajectory).

Step 5 — Hybrid Merging: For queries that include both trajectory and point-in-
time components, the trajectory sequence is merged with standard similarity-
ranked chunks from the broader patient record. Merged results are deduped and
ordered chronologically before presentation to the generation model.

5.4 Phase 4: Temporal Context Construction and Trajectory-Aware Generation

The context window presented to the language model is restructured to make
temporal ordering explicit. Rather than presenting chunks in relevance-ranked
order with generic headers, TempoRAG constructs a temporal narrative context:

    === CLINICAL TRAJECTORY: Creatinine (Kidney Function) — Jan 2024 to Jan 2025 ===

    [2024-01-15]  Lab Result — Creatinine: 1.8 mg/dL  (Reference: 0.7-1.2)  [ELEVATED]
    [2024-04-20]  Lab Result — Creatinine: 2.1 mg/dL  (Reference: 0.7-1.2)  [HIGH]
    [2024-06-05]  Clinical Note — Nephrology referral initiated due to rising creatinine.
    [2024-09-18]  Lab Result — Creatinine: 1.9 mg/dL  (Reference: 0.7-1.2)  [ELEVATED]
    [2025-01-08]  Lab Result — Creatinine: 1.7 mg/dL  (Reference: 0.7-1.2)  [ELEVATED]

    Trend direction: ↑ Rise (Jan-Apr 2024), then ↓ Partial improvement (Apr 2024–Jan 2025)
    Net change over period: +0.1 mg/dL from baseline

This structured temporal context provides the language model with the
information required to produce accurate trend descriptions, comparisons, and
causal assessments. The generation prompt is adapted with explicit instructions
for temporal reasoning:

    "You are reviewing the following ordered clinical timeline for a patient.
     Based solely on the records provided, describe the clinical trajectory
     and answer the patient's question. Explicitly state whether values are
     increasing, decreasing, or stable, and over what time period."

System prompt design follows best practices for grounded clinical generation
identified in the medical LLM literature (Singhal et al., 2023; Zakka et al.,
2024), including strict instructions against extrapolation beyond the provided
records and mandatory uncertainty disclosure.

5.5 Phase 5: Benchmark Dataset — ClinicalTrajQA

A prerequisite contribution of this research is the creation of ClinicalTrajQA,
a benchmark dataset for evaluating temporal trajectory reasoning in healthcare
RAG systems. ClinicalTrajQA is constructed from the MIMIC-IV dataset (Johnson
et al., 2023), which provides de-identified EHR data from over 300,000 ICU
and non-ICU patient admissions at the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center.

Dataset construction proceeds as follows:

    Step 1 — Patient Selection: Identify patients in MIMIC-IV with longitudinal
    records spanning at least 12 months and at least three measurements of one
    or more key clinical indicators (creatinine, HbA1c, LDL cholesterol, blood
    pressure, haemoglobin, eGFR).

    Step 2 — Temporal Pattern Annotation: For each selected patient and
    indicator, compute the ground-truth temporal pattern: trend direction
    (monotone increasing, monotone decreasing, non-monotone), net change,
    clinically significant transition points (abnormal → normal, normal →
    critical), and duration of abnormality episodes.

    Step 3 — Question Generation: Construct natural language questions covering
    all five query types (trend, comparison, duration, causal-temporal,
    point-in-time) for each annotated patient-indicator pair, using templated
    generation followed by manual revision to ensure naturalness.

    Step 4 — Answer Annotation: Generate ground-truth answers using the
    computed temporal patterns. Answers are validated by two clinical domain
    experts (physicians or clinical pharmacists) who confirm clinical accuracy.

    Step 5 — Dataset Statistics: Target minimum 2,500 question-answer pairs,
    balanced across five query types and four clinical domains (renal function,
    metabolic, cardiovascular, haematological), with train/validation/test splits
    of 70/15/15.

ClinicalTrajQA will be released publicly under an institutional data use
agreement compatible with MIMIC-IV's PhysioNet licensing terms, providing the
research community with a replicable evaluation resource.

6. EVALUATION FRAMEWORK
================================================================================

TempoRAG will be evaluated across three dimensions:

6.1 Retrieval Quality

Retrieval quality is evaluated on ClinicalTrajQA using standard information
retrieval metrics adapted for temporal queries:

    - Temporal Recall@K: the fraction of ground-truth records within the
      target time window that appear in the top-K retrieved chunks
    - Temporal Order Accuracy: the proportion of retrieved sequences where
      chronological ordering is preserved
    - Time Window Coverage: the fraction of the query's specified temporal
      interval covered by the retrieved records' timestamps
    - Standard Precision@K and nDCG@K for content relevance

Baseline comparison: TempoRAG trajectory retrieval versus (a) standard
cosine similarity RAG; (b) time-decay-weighted RAG (as implemented in systems
such as the one described in this proposal's motivating project); (c) BM25
keyword retrieval; (d) GraphRAG (Edge et al., 2024) applied to clinical text.

6.2 Generation Quality

Generation quality is evaluated using a combination of automated metrics and
human clinical evaluation:

Automated metrics:
    - Temporal Accuracy Score (TAS): a novel metric assessing whether the
      generated response correctly identifies trend direction, magnitude, and
      time period. TAS is computed by parsing the generated response for
      temporal claims and comparing against the ground-truth temporal pattern.
    - Faithfulness (RAGAS framework, Es et al., 2024): proportion of factual
      claims in the response that are grounded in retrieved context
    - Answer Relevancy (RAGAS): semantic similarity of the response to the
      ground-truth answer
    - ROUGE-L and BERTScore against reference answers

Human evaluation:
    - Clinical accuracy: rated by domain experts on a 5-point Likert scale
    - Temporal correctness: expert assessment of whether trend descriptions
      are clinically accurate
    - Utility: patient-facing assessment of response helpfulness

6.3 Safety Evaluation

Given the clinical deployment context, a dedicated safety evaluation assesses:
    - Hallucination rate: claims in the response not grounded in retrieved
      records, assessed via entailment models and human review
    - Temporal hallucination rate: specifically, incorrect trend directions,
      dates, or durations generated beyond what the records support
    - Calibration: whether the system's expressed uncertainty aligns with
      actual accuracy rates

7. EXPECTED CONTRIBUTIONS
================================================================================

This research is expected to produce the following original contributions to
the field:

    C1 — TempoRAG Framework: A complete, open-source implementation of the
         Temporal Clinical Trajectory-Aware RAG architecture, including the
         temporal chunk graph, trajectory retrieval mechanism, and temporal
         context construction components.

    C2 — ClinicalTrajQA Benchmark: A publicly available, clinician-validated
         benchmark dataset of 2,500+ temporal trajectory QA pairs from MIMIC-IV,
         representing the first dedicated evaluation resource for temporal
         reasoning in healthcare RAG.

    C3 — Temporal Accuracy Score (TAS): A novel automated evaluation metric
         for assessing temporal trajectory reasoning in generated clinical
         responses, validated against human clinical expert judgement.

    C4 — Empirical Evidence: Systematic experimental evidence on the conditions
         under which temporal trajectory retrieval improves over standard RAG
         baselines, including analysis of query type, clinical domain, and
         record density effects.

    C5 — Clinical Deployment Guidelines: A set of evidence-based guidelines
         for safe deployment of trajectory-aware RAG in clinical and patient-
         facing health information systems, addressing temporal hallucination
         risk, uncertainty communication, and edge case handling.

The primary publication target is top venues in the intersection of NLP and
medical informatics: ACL, EMNLP, NAACL, the Journal of the American Medical
Informatics Association (JAMIA), npj Digital Medicine, and the ACM Conference
on Health, Inference, and Learning (CHIL).

9. ETHICAL CONSIDERATIONS
================================================================================

This research operates with de-identified EHR data from MIMIC-IV, which is
publicly available under a PhysioNet Credentialed Health Data License. All data
use will comply with the terms of this license and with applicable data
protection regulations (GDPR in the European context; HIPAA in the United
States context). The proposed ClinicalTrajQA dataset will include only data
already de-identified under MIMIC-IV's IRB-approved protocol.

The research acknowledges the dual-use risk of clinical AI systems. TempoRAG
is designed for use under clinical or patient supervision, not as a replacement
for clinical judgement. All generated responses in the deployed system must
carry explicit disclaimers, consistent with responsible AI deployment standards
articulated in Rajpurkar et al. (2022) and Moor et al. (2023). The uncertainty
quantification component of the evaluation framework (Section 6.3) directly
addresses the risk of overconfident temporal claims that could mislead patients.

Expert clinical reviewers involved in ClinicalTrajQA annotation will receive
appropriate compensation and their contributions will be acknowledged in all
publications resulting from use of the dataset.

10. SIGNIFICANCE OF THE RESEARCH
================================================================================

Chronic disease management is the dominant challenge in contemporary healthcare
systems. Over 60% of adults in high-income countries live with at least one
chronic condition, and the management of chronic conditions is fundamentally
longitudinal — it is predicated on tracking changes in clinical indicators
across time and adjusting treatment in response to observed trajectories
(Wornow et al., 2023; Rajpurkar et al., 2022). Natural language interfaces
to EHR data represent a powerful tool for enabling patients and clinicians to
interact with this longitudinal record, yet current RAG-based interfaces are
structurally incapable of supporting the temporal reasoning that chronic disease
management requires.

TempoRAG addresses this gap by introducing temporal trajectory reasoning as
a first-class concern in healthcare RAG system design. If successful, this
research will provide both a practical system that can be deployed in clinical
and patient-facing settings to improve longitudinal health communication, and
a theoretical framework for understanding temporal retrieval as a distinct
problem from semantic similarity retrieval — a distinction with broad
implications beyond the healthcare domain, extending to legal document
reasoning, financial record analysis, and any application domain where the
sequence of events matters as much as the content of individual records.

APPENDIX A: GLOSSARY OF KEY TERMS
================================================================================

Clinical Trajectory    A temporally ordered sequence of clinical measurements,
                       events, or observations that together characterise the
                       progression of a patient's health state over time.

EHR                    Electronic Health Record. A digital record of a patient's
                       medical history, including diagnoses, medications,
                       laboratory results, imaging reports, and clinical notes.

RAG                    Retrieval-Augmented Generation. An architecture combining
                       a retrieval system (which fetches relevant documents from
                       an external corpus) with a generative language model
                       (which produces natural language outputs conditioned on
                       the retrieved documents).

Temporal Relation      A relation between two clinical events or time expressions
                       that specifies their relative ordering (e.g., BEFORE,
                       AFTER, SIMULTANEOUS, DURING). Defined formally in the
                       TimeML specification (Styler et al., 2014).

TempoRAG               The proposed framework: Temporal Clinical Trajectory-
                       Aware Retrieval-Augmented Generation.

ClinicalTrajQA         The proposed benchmark dataset: Clinical Trajectory
                       Question Answering, constructed from MIMIC-IV.

TAS                    Temporal Accuracy Score. A proposed evaluation metric
                       assessing the correctness of temporal trajectory claims
                       in generated clinical responses.

MMR                    Maximal Marginal Relevance. A reranking criterion that
                       balances document relevance with diversity, preventing
                       redundant retrieval results.

MIMIC-IV               Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV. A large,
                       freely available EHR database from the Beth Israel
                       Deaconess Medical Center, maintained by PhysioNet
                       (Johnson et al., 2023).
